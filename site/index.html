Runoff night, minute by minute

Brazil counts electronic ballots fast and publishes results by polling section. The catch: sections don't arrive in a random order. Early on, the running national tally is dominated by whichever states report first, so it can point the wrong way for an hour or more.

The model corrects for that. It projects each municipality's final result from the sections already in, using only information available before the night (for the runoff, each municipality's first-round result) to fill in what hasn't reported. The raw tally is what the official results page showed. The market is the Polymarket price.

At 19:04 on runoff night, with 1.5% of sections counted, the model put Lula's chance of winning at 0.95; the market said 0.38. The market crossed 0.5 at 19:10. Then from 19:45 to 20:23, between 14% and 46% counted, the raw tally showed Flávio ahead, and the market fell from 0.86 to 0.62 while the model's projection barely moved. The market settled above 0.9 at 20:24, the same minute the raw tally turned back to Lula. He won 51.79–48.21.

The market seems to have been reading the raw tally, not the count underneath it.

Is that just noise in the price?

It's a fair worry. The gap and the move both contain the current price, so a stale or bouncing price alone would produce a positive slope even in a market that priced the count perfectly. The checks below each break that link in a different way. If noise were the story, the effect would vanish when the move is measured from a minute later, and the placebos would show the same slope as the model. Neither happens.

Every race I could replay

Ten races, three elections. For 2026 the comparison is with the market. For 2022 there are no Polymarket prices left, so the comparison is with the raw tally the market appears to follow. Each 2026 runoff replay ran once, with settings frozen before its data was downloaded.

Could you have traded it?

Barely. Using every real trade on Polymarket after each signal, here is what could have been bought within 30 minutes at the signal price, or by paying a little more:

On the first round, 98% of the fillable amount was in one market in its first four minutes. On the runoff, the minute price history lagged real trades by 2–4¢, so nothing traded at the price the signal saw. The information gap is real; the money in it is small and goes fast.

Beyond Brazil

Is Brazil a case or a pattern? Before looking at held-out data I registered three tests across ten Polymarket categories, each with its hypothesis, sample and statistic hashed in advance and run once:

  • Thin markets over-react to sharp moves and then revert: significant in 6 of 10 categories after multiple-testing correction, same sign in all ten. pre-registered
  • "Mentions" markets under-price near-certain outcomes: +2.1% per dollar after spread and fees, but only $8.3k fillable across 1,851 entries. pre-registered
  • Deep markets reacting too slowly in general: not supported. pre-registered

So the slow pricing on Brazil's count nights is a specific case, not something this sample finds across deep markets.

How much to trust each piece

  • in-sample The model's settings were chosen on the first-round night. It shows the method can work, not that it will.
  • pre-specified Settings frozen before the data was examined, each run once. Outcomes were already known when the replays ran.
  • pre-registered Tests fixed and hashed before seeing held-out data; the hashes and every look are in the repository.

What would change my mind: a live, forward-recorded count night where the gap stops predicting the price. The tool runs live (brunder night) and is being tested on upcoming elections.
